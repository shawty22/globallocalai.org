Local AI Under Human Control

How can communities share local AI affordably, and how can operators isolate an AI system while keeping an essential service running safely?

These ten questions grow out of the GLAMifesto. The research library provides background. Below we distinguish existing evidence from tests we propose and the results that would change our approach.

Research track

Community Compute

Study the cost, access rules, privacy and practical limits of shared local and hybrid AI.

Existing evidence. Berkeley Lab documents data-center energy and water use. The Virginia legislative audit examines siting, noise and employment. These provide comparison points for community-scale measurements. Berkeley Lab report · Virginia legislative audit

  1. What does shared local compute cost in power, water and space?

    Compare comparable workloads across local and cloud configurations, including idle power and cooling. A local setup that draws more resources for the same accepted work would weaken the case for that workload.

  2. Can a shared node operate without disturbing neighbors?

    Measure normal and peak noise in occupied spaces, including at night. If an agreed limit is exceeded, change the equipment, placement or hours before proposing a pilot.

  3. Which operating and training work actually stays local?

    Track paid maintenance, support and training hours. If work mainly accrues to outside vendors, revisit staffing and handover.

  4. Which everyday tasks can smaller models handle?

    Compare task acceptance, retries and energy across local and cloud options. Shift the routing boundary when a model misses an agreed quality threshold.

  5. What can leak from a local or hybrid deployment?

    Use synthetic records to check outbound traffic, access between users and deletion. Evidence of leakage would change defaults for logging, storage or routing.

  6. Can people enforce the rules they set for their knowledge?

    With willing rights holders, test retrieval, reuse, export and withdrawal using material they authorize. A failed permission or withdrawal test would block wider use of that material.

  7. Which hardware and access rules make a shared node affordable and usable?

    Compare cost per user, queue time and accessibility across configurations. If some groups face consistently worse access, revisit allocation rules and support.

  8. When does reusing a checked public answer help?

    Compare exact matches, semantic matches and retrieval of source documents. Count stale answers and avoided cloud calls. Tighten reuse rules if error or staleness exceeds the agreed limit.

  9. Can another community reproduce the results?

    Share permitted test inputs, settings and results. If the outcome depends on one site or device, document that boundary and test again.

Research track

Critical Operations

Study whether a human operator can isolate an AI system while an essential service remains safe and under independent control.

Existing evidence. NIST documents the safety and availability requirements of operational technology. Joint guidance for infrastructure operators calls for practicing isolation of vital systems while maintaining service. NIST operational technology guide · CI Fortify isolation guidance

  1. Can an operator isolate an AI system and keep the service running safely?

    Begin with synthetic pump telemetry and an isolated simulation. Test a cutoff outside the AI host. Observe whether test commands stop at the boundary and whether the simulated service stays in its safe range under independent human control.

    Any command that crosses after cutoff, any unsafe state, or any failed manual recovery would change the design. Record what was tested and the limits of the result.

Help us improve the tests

We welcome conversations with researchers, operators and community hosts. Bring a comparable study, a measurement we’ve missed, or a condition under which you think a proposal would fail.
